# Finding the idea

The idea is one sentence a stranger could repeat after watching once. If you cannot write it, you
do not have one yet, and no amount of springs will hide that.

## The line

`It's <something from outside the product's category> for <what this product really does>.`

## Patterns that have worked

| Pattern | Shape | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Borrowed scene** | A famous film or TV scene replayed by the product's own characters | The product has a cast or mascot and the scene's power dynamic matches the mechanic (a pitch call for a trading swarm) |
| **The clone / the agent does it** | A character launches something that then acts on its own while they do nothing | Anything autonomous: agents, bots, automation |
| **Mechanic as creature behaviour** | Units of the product hatch, swarm, feed, die on screen | Products with spawning, supply, burning, growth loops |
| **Native medium** | The whole film is made of the product's own material: ASCII, receipts, a chat feed, a terminal | The product already has a strong texture; do not add a second one |
| **One object's journey** | A single thing (a pack, a file, a coin) is followed from start to payoff | Linear mechanics: post → open → receive |
| **The proof wall** | One real item, then hundreds of real items | The product has real inventory, users or outputs to show |
| **Receipt** | A real event rendered as a short, data-driven card or clip | Recurring events: wins, payouts, spawns |

Mixes are fine. Most good ones are a borrowed scene or a character carrying a mechanic.

## Things that are not ideas

- A question in bold ("You built it. Now what?")
- "Kinetic statements": three verbs with full stops
- A camera moving over a screenshot
- A grid that fills up while a counter runs
- "Problem → solution → features → logo" with nothing happening inside the scenes

These are layouts. A layout can carry an idea but cannot replace one.

## When the product has no world

No logo, no mascot, no UI. Do not fall back to an off-white page with one accent colour.

1. Take the one concrete noun the product is about (a file, a pack, a dot, a key).
2. Give it a body or a face using its own shapes, not added cartoon eyes on a logo.
3. Decide what it wants. The video is it getting that.

Describe the character in the approval message. Once the concept is approved, draw a reference sheet
(front, three-quarter, side) and get that approved before any scene.

The "not reusable" test is hardest here. Pass it with the product's specifics: its real command,
its real count, the real names of the places or things it touches.

## Turning the product's truth into the idea

Ask in order, stop at the first good answer:

1. What does the product do that would be funny or dramatic if a person did it?
2. What scene from a film has the same power dynamic?
3. What does the mechanic look like as a physical place: a floor, a hive, a vault, a desk?
4. What is the one real number or real asset that makes people stop? Build the moment around it.
5. What does the audience already call this, in their own slang? Use that, not marketing words.

## Slogans

Offer three to five, grounded in the audience's own references, and say which you would pick.
Never present an invented line as the project's official slogan. If the community already has a
tagline, twist theirs rather than writing a new one.

## The user is the director

Expect the best concept to come from the person, half-formed, in one line. Your job is to hear it,
sharpen it and say back exactly what will happen on screen. When a draft is called "not it", ask
what the idea was supposed to be before changing anything visual: the fix is usually the idea
being unclear, not the animation.
